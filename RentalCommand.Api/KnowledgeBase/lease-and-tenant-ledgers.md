A tenant ledger is the running money history for one rental account. It answers a practical question: **what was charged, what was paid or credited, and what is still due?**

## What the tenant sees

A tenant sees their own balance and account history. That can include rent charges, other agreed charges, payments received, credits, refunds, corrections, the next amount due, and the security deposit held.

Tenants do not see your accounting account codes, debit and credit columns, management notes, other tenants, or other units.

## What landlords, owners, and managers see

Authorized staff see the same tenant balance with the detail needed to manage it. Depending on their access, they can review due dates, open charges, payment records, credits, recurring charges, supporting documents, and the accounting record behind an item.

Access still follows the properties and work each person is allowed to manage. An owner or manager does not automatically see every rental in the workspace.

## Charges, payments, and credits

- A **charge** adds an amount the tenant owes, such as rent, a late fee, parking, or a documented repair charge.
- A **payment** records money received and reduces what the tenant owes when it is applied to charges.
- A **credit** reduces an amount owed without pretending cash was received. It can correct an overcharge or recognize an agreed adjustment.

The original entry stays in the history when a correction is made. That makes it possible to explain how the balance changed instead of silently rewriting the past.

## Recurring and one-time charges

A recurring charge is a schedule for creating future charges, such as monthly rent or parking. A one-time charge covers a specific event, such as a replacement key or utility reimbursement.

Setting up a recurring charge does not erase prior charges. Each posted charge becomes its own dated ledger entry.

## Deposits are held separately

A security deposit is money held for the tenant, not rent income. Rental Command shows the deposit held beside the tenant ledger, but it does not add that amount to rent due or use it to make the tenant's receivable balance look smaller.

Keeping deposits separate helps you see two different obligations clearly: what the tenant owes you and what deposit money you may owe back to the tenant.
