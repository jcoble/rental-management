The general ledger is the complete, organized history behind your books. It groups every recorded money event into accounts such as cash, rent income, repairs, tenant balances, deposits owed back, and loans.

## Why it is different from Activity

Activity is the day-to-day list of payments, expenses, deposits, and withdrawals. The general ledger shows how each of those events affected the books.

For example, a rent payment can increase cash and reduce the amount the tenant owes. Those two effects belong to one balanced accounting record.

## Double-entry in plain words

Double-entry bookkeeping means every recorded event explains both sides of what changed. Money did not simply appear or disappear.

Examples:

- Receiving rent increases cash and reduces rent still owed.
- Paying a repair bill increases repair expense and decreases cash.
- Receiving a security deposit increases deposit cash and increases the amount held for the tenant.
- Paying loan principal decreases cash and decreases the loan balance.

Accountants call the two sides **debits and credits**. You do not need to choose those sides during ordinary work. Rental Command records them from the payment, charge, expense, deposit, or other action you completed.

## Why each account has its own balance

Cash, income, expenses, loans, and deposits mean different things, so the full general ledger does not have one useful running balance. Choose one category or account to see how its balance changed over time.

Simple mode labels the effect as an increase or decrease. Advanced mode shows the debit and credit columns and account codes.

## Why the ledger matters

The general ledger connects daily records to financial statements. It lets you or your accountant trace a total back to the dated entries and then to the original payment, expense, charge, or document.

That trace is what makes organized books explainable. It does not replace professional tax or accounting advice, but it gives that professional a clearer record to review.
